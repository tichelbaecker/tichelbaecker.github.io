Research

My research spans three interconnected areas. I study the composition, emergence, and evolution of political elites and party organizations, the nature and causes of political polarization, and how conflict, violence, and extremist actors threaten liberal democracy. Across these areas, I combine large-scale archival data with experimental and observational research designs.

Who becomes a politician — and why?

Political representation begins with who enters politics in the first place. I study the composition of political elites — examining how individual characteristics, local context, and voter discrimination shape who stands for office and who succeeds. My work leverages newly collected candidate data across all levels of government (national, state, local).

How do parties select and recruit candidates?

Parties play a central role in the selection and recruitment of candidates. I investigate how parties select and recruit candidates, how nomination strategies respond to electoral incentives, and how the interplay between political demand and supply determines the pool of representatives. Drawing on novel candidate-level data from local elections, I trace these dynamics at a granular level.

How do local elites help new parties in establishing organizations?

My dissertation, Taking Root, asks how new political parties build durable local organizations. I show that new parties mobilize voters and establish roots in communities by recruiting local elites who leverage their existing ties — transforming party entry from a top-down directive into a bottom-up process.

What drives polarization?

Citizens across democracies increasingly view political opponents with distrust and hostility. I study how people perceive and experience these divides — examining whether partisan animosity reflects genuine disagreement or distorted perceptions of the other side.

How can we measure polarization?

Most work focuses on survey-based methods to understand polarization in the mass public. In work together with Lea Portmann, I study electoral behavior that can help us to understand the context in which de-polarized behavior takes place.

Who (de-)polarizes and why?

Previous work has shown that elites play a powerful role in shaping polarization of ordinary citizens. Building on this, I investigate which type of elites polarize and under which circumstances elites are able to reach out to voters that are ideologically distant.

What fuels support for extremist actors in liberal democracies?

I study how and when far-right parties are able to attract support in liberal democracies scrutinizing the role of domestic out-migration and gender imbalances. I also try to understand how and when far-right parties can build an increasingly loyal electorate.

How do war and violence shape political behavior?

War and political violence leave lasting imprints on democratic politics. I study how exposure to conflict — whether through wartime casualties, political repression, or communal violence — reshapes citizens’ political attitudes and behavior long after the fighting ends. I also study how political violence in established democracies shape the composition of political elites.

How can liberal democracies safeguard institutions?

Focusing on the case of Germany, I analyze the tools that militant democracies have already today to their disposal in fending off challenges undermining state institutions. In particular, I focus on how state surveillance and the threat of disciplinary consequences can deter civil servants to join extremist parties.
